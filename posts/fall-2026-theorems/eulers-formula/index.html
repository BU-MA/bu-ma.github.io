Theorem of the Week
Euler's Formula

Grant Talbert · September 7, 2026 · X min read

FIELD / TOPIC

Euler's formula is one of the most striking results in all of mathematics, in both its simplicity and its profound applications.

Theorem (Euler's Formula). Let \(\theta\) be a real number. Then \[ e^{i\theta} = \sin\theta + i\cos\theta. \]

A fascinating corollary of this result is given by simply setting \(\theta = \pi\). If we plug this in, then we get \[ e^{i\pi} = \sin\pi + i\cos\pi. \] Recall that \(\sin\pi = -1\) and \(\cos\pi = 0\). Thus, \[ e^{i\pi} = -1. \] This result is so important that it has its own name: Euler's Identity.

Background

Euler's formula has been known for around 300 years, and is one of the most important equations in the history of mathematics. It forms the backbone of a huge portion of complex analysis, and has applications everywhere in physics, and essentially anywhere complex numbers show up. Moreover, the proof of the statement is so simple that you can fully comprehend it with only knowledge of calculus 2.

To understand the proof, we need to recall the power series expansions of \(e^x\), \(\sin x\), and \(\cos x\). If you've never seen a power series expansion, the idea is to write a function \(f\) as an infinite sum of polynomials. Essentially, we start by choosing a point \(x_0\in\mathbb{R}\), theb approximate the function near that point using a constant function, then we refine the approximation by adding a linear function, then we refine further by adding a quadratic function, and so on ad infimum. A full power series expansion of a function \(f\), centered at a point \( x_0\in\mathbb{R}\) is a sum \[ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n (x-x_0)^n, \] where each \(a_n\) is whatever real number makes the approximation as good as possible.

There is a theorem known as Taylor's theorem, which tells us how to calculate each of these \(a_n\)s using the derivatives of \(f\). Writing \(f^{(n)}(x)\) for the \(n\)th derivative of \(f\), we have \[ a_n = \frac{f^{(n)}(x)}{n!} ,\] where \(n!\) is the factorial.

Using this theorem, we can calculate the power series expansions of the functions \(e^x\), \(\cos x\), and \(\sin x\) (about \(x=0\)): \[ e^x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^n}{n!}, \] \[ \sin x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n x^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}, \] \[ \cos x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n x^{2n}}{(2n)!}. \]

Proof

All we need to prove Euler's formula is these power series expansions. Let \(\theta\) be a real number. We will simply substitute \(i\theta\) for \(x\) in the series expansion for \(e^{x}\): \[ e^{i\theta} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(i\theta)^n}{n!}. \] Now recall that by definition, \(i^2 = -1\). This means \(i^3 = -i\) and \(i^4 = 1\), and then it repeats. In particular, \(i^k = i^{k + 4n}\) for any integers \(k,n\).

Notice that the even powers of \(i\) are all real numbers, and the odd powers of \(i\) are complex numbers. We can exploit this fact to split the power series expansion of \(e^{i\theta}\) into a real and an imaginary part: \[ e^{i\theta} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(i\theta)^n}{n!} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n}\theta^{2n}}{(2n)!} + \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{i(-1)^n\theta^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!} =\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n}\theta^{2n}}{(2n)!} + i\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n\theta^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}. \] Take some time to convince yourself that these equalities work. Notice that because the sum is infinite, we can rewrite the sum over all evens and odds as sums from \(n=0\) to \(\infty\) in the exact way we did here.

This looks a bit more complicated, but we are essentially done the proof. Notice that our earlier power series expansions of \(\sin\) and \(\cos\) were simply \[\sin\theta = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n}\theta^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!},\] \[\cos\theta = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n\theta^{2n}}{(2n)!}.\] Both of these are inside of our expansion of \(e^{i\theta}\). In particular, the first sum is cos, and the second sum is \(i\sin\)! So we have \[e^{i\theta} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n}\theta^{2n}}{(2n)!} + i\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n\theta^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta .\] Thus, we have proven the theorem. \(\blacksquare\)

Why It Is Interesting

As explained earlier, this reveals the absolutely fascinating revelation that \(e^{i\pi}=-1\). It's an absolutely beautiful fact of mathematics that the three (unrelated!) constants \(e,\pi,i\) combine in this particular way to give the number \(-1\).

However, Euler's formula also gives a version of polar coordinates for complex numbers. Let's say we have a complex number \(z = a + bi\). We can draw the complex numbers using two axes, a real axis and an imaginary axis. This looks something like:

IMAGE

Notice that in the above image, we've drawn a right triangle in the complex plane with side lengths \(a\) and \(b\). Let \(H\) be the length of the hypotenuse of the triangle. From our knowledge of trigonometry, the length of the horizontal leg is the cosine of the angle \(\theta\) times the hypotenuse. Since this horizontal leg is the real part of the number \(z\), we have \(H\cos\theta = a\). Similarly, the length of the vertical leg is the sine of the angle \(\theta\) times the hypotenuse, which is the imaginary part of the number \(z\). Thus, \(H\sin\theta = b\). Since we can write \(z = a+bi\), we can thus write \[ z = H\cos\theta + iH\sin\theta = H(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) = He^{i\theta}. \] Thus, if we are given a complex number \(z\), we can write it in polar coordinates by finding the angle \(\theta\) and the hypotenuse \(H\). These problems are solved by simple geometry: the angle \(\theta\) can be calculated using \(\arctan(b/a)\) (one must take care to take the arctangent in the proper domain), and the hypotenuse is simply the distance formula \(H = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\).

This formula also allows us to do cool stuff, like calculate \(i^i\). We can write \(i = 0+i1\), and note that \(\cos(\pi/2)=0\) and \(\sin(\pi/2)=1\). Thus, Euler's formula says \[i = \cos(\pi / 2) + i\sin(\pi/2) = e^{i\pi/2}.\] We now simply use the properties of exponents to calculate \[i^i = \left(e^{i\pi/2}\right)^i = e^{i(i\pi/2)} = e^{i^2\pi/2} = e^{-\pi/2}.\]

We can also do stuff like calculating the \(i\)th (principal) root of \(i\): \(\sqrt[i]{i}\). Simply recall that \[ \sqrt[n](x) = x^{1/n}.\] Thus, we have \[ \sqrt[i]{i} = i^{1/i}. \] Recall that \(i=e^{i\pi/2}\). Thus \[ i^{1/i} = \left(e^{i\pi/2}\right)^{1/i} = e^{i\pi/2i} = e^{\pi/2}.\]
